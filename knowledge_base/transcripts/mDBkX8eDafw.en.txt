[00:01] INTRO
Nude photography is a popular form of people photography. The nude body has beautiful
forms and has been an inexhaustible source of inspiration for good photographs for more
than 170 years. It is certainly not the easiest hobby. When you take your first steps down
this path you often don't know how to deal with nudity. How do you photograph your model
well? Are your photos not erotic — where is that line? And how do you find models? The
studio is a SAFE place to take those first steps: you have complete control of the lighting
and you have privacy, so you can work calmly together with your model. On this DVD Raoul
Nijhorst teaches you his best techniques.

[01:35] FINDING MODELS
Ask someone you have already photographed to sit for you again, or approach someone
directly — and tell them they may bring someone along: a parent, a partner, anyone who
makes the model more comfortable. Be aware a chaperone can also work against the
atmosphere; start with the person present, and once there is a good rapport the model may
say herself that she'll come alone, which can work better.
You can also find models online — social platforms, or a dedicated model website. Approach
someone and say: "I'm a photographer, I'd like to try some nude work."
Important: HAVE A PORTFOLIO. Then you can show the person what you have already made.
It does not have to be brilliant immediately — the point is that they see you are working
seriously, that you are not just someone who picked up a camera to take "nice pictures of
naked women."

[03:02] THE GOLDEN RULES ON SET
Photographing a nude model asks more of you than an average model shoot.
- Do not leave your model standing unnecessarily undressed for long periods.
- When the model needs to fix something — adjust her hair, correct a pose — TURN AWAY.
  Do not stand there staring.
- Treat the model with respect at all times. Keep a good working relationship.
- No sexist remarks. Keep it correct.
- Do not touch your model. And do not make advances on your model.
- Stay professional at all times.
- I am against alcohol on set. I understand wanting to loosen the model up, to put her at
  ease — but you should do that with your social skills. Talk to her. Be interested. If you
  create a good atmosphere on set, the rest follows by itself.

[04:02] BE READY BEFORE SHE UNDRESSES
Besides handling your model well, handle your equipment well. Have everything standing
ready: lamps positioned, lighting correct, light METERED, and the camera configured.
Nothing is worse than the model standing there undressed while you are still wrestling with
your camera settings and she has to wait the whole time.
If you don't yet know what you are going to do, REHEARSE DRY first. By dry I mean: let her
keep her clothes on, set the pose, and check that everything is right. Once the pose is good
and the lighting is good, you can ask the model to undress, and then take the photograph.

[04:54] THE CONTRACT
After a nude shoot it is good to work from a contract. A contract puts every agreement in
black and white so you cannot easily run into problems later — suppose there is a jealous
boyfriend, or you fall out with the model; at least you already made agreements in writing.
What is in my contract:
- The model may use the photos for her portfolio, PROVIDED the photographer is credited.
  It is right that the photographer's name is always mentioned.
- Conversely, the photographer may use the photos for his or her portfolio.
- NEITHER party may simply sell the photos. No commercial purpose — purely own use.
[Model signs on camera.]
TIP: To find a good nude model it helps to already have a good nude portfolio. To avoid the
chicken-and-egg problem, first attend a few nude photography workshops, where you get
familiar with this branch of photography in a trusted environment and can build a series of
images to show a model.

[05:59] LIGHTING — HARD VS SOFT
With nude work good lighting is essential. If you use bad light it can make your model look
less flattering. Just as with your camera, the POSITION of your lamps is very important — it
determines the mood of the photograph.
Here is a lamp with an enormous reflector on it (there is a fan inside for cooling). With this
reflector I get HARD light. How can you see the light is hard? By the shadow cast behind
Nadia — hard contours, the whole shadow sharply drawn. You can also see it in the shadows
on her face and body. Hard light is less flattering for a model, so I don't like using it. You can
use it for certain photographs, but I prefer to work with softboxes.
Here is the softbox. With a softbox you get soft light — the light on Nadia is beautifully soft,
and you can see the shadow is practically absent. Very flattering for your model.

[07:50] THE GOVERNING PRINCIPLE
You are inclined to think: "Softbox, so I can put my lamp anywhere, the light is always soft."
That is not entirely true. I will show you that you can make HARD light with a softbox too.
Watch — I move the lamp back, and now a shadow has appeared on Nadia.
The reason: THE SIZE OF YOUR LIGHT SOURCE DETERMINES HOW HARD OR SOFT YOUR
LIGHT IS — and I mean the size of your source RELATIVE TO THE SUBJECT. Because I had
the softbox very close to Nadia you got soft light. By moving the lamp further away, the
lamp becomes very small relative to Nadia.
Compare it to the sun. The sun is enormous. But standing here, the sun is actually a tiny
point. THAT is why the sun is a hard light source — you notice it walking outside in the
middle of the day: very hard shadows.

[08:45] STRIPLIGHT AND RIM LIGHT
Here is another softbox — this is called a striplight. What is good about it: it is long and
narrow, this one is 1.80m. A normal small softbox gives lovely light on the upper half of the
model — but of course you also want to photograph the lower half. The striplight solves that
because it is so long: you can light the whole body.
I also use a striplight from BEHIND, which gives a rim light effect — light running along the
edge of the model. That lets the model separate from the background, and you can create
beautiful effects.
Notice that the position of your lamp really matters: we just went from mysterious to fully
lit, and we changed nothing except position.

[10:01] GRIDS AND SNOOTS — THE HARDNESS LADDER
Here is a lamp with a reflector, and on that reflector a GRID. Like the reflector it bundles the
light and gives hard light — but the grid makes it HARDER still.
Next: this reflector is called a SNOOT. A snoot concentrates the light even more, and this
snoot also has a grid in it, so you get harder light again — really hard shadows.
Usually you would NOT aim a snoot straight at the model. You use it as a hair light, or when
you want to accent particular parts of the body. You get beautiful effects with it.

[10:40] BEAUTY DISH
Here is a beauty dish. What is good about this light: because it comes from an angle of
roughly 45 degrees above and sits squarely in front of Nadia, she is lit SYMMETRICALLY,
which accentuates her jawline nicely. And because it is a ROUND source you get round
catchlights in the eyes, whereas a rectangular softbox leaves rectangles.
What you do get is a hard shadow under the chin. I fill that with a reflector board — watch,
I take the board and the shadow disappears. I'll show it again: away, and back.
Someone might say "Raoul, you're doing a portrait now." We are working with the whole
body, but that's fine — you can always make a half-body shot and use the beauty dish
beautifully there too. If you find the fill too strong, use the other side of the reflector board;
this is softer. So you can simply play with your board.

[12:35] COMBINING THE LIGHTS + CONTINUOUS VS FLASH
Now a combined setup: a striplight on this side, a striplight on the other side, a reflector
with grid aimed at the background, and a beauty dish with the reflector board beneath it.
You may have noticed the light is quite present — that is because I work with CONTINUOUS
light. Many photographers work with flash. Personally I find continuous more pleasant, but
it makes no difference at all to the photograph. So don't think you have to run to the shop
and buy continuous lamps. Everything I am telling you here can be achieved exactly the
same with flash. For me personally: it's pleasantly warm on set, and it is what-you-see-is-
what-you-get, no surprises afterwards.
[Directs Nadia — hand near the face, hands beneath the bust, a different gaze.]
TIP: Do not buy a whole battery of lamps at once. First try to get the maximum out of ONE
lamp. Once you truly understand the light, add a lamp. And maybe another.

[14:07] VIEWPOINT, TETHERING, BOOM LIGHT
As a beginning photographer you tend to shoot from one standpoint, but it is more
interesting to vary it. I am standing on a ladder now, shooting from a higher viewpoint.
You can judge the photo on the camera screen, but here I have a cable running to the laptop
— this is called TETHERED shooting, so I see the result immediately on the monitor. That is
good for the photographer and for the model. Or the makeup artist can watch along and see
the result at once.
You may also have noticed a lamp above — a large softbox for soft light, mounted on a stand;
this is called a BOOM LIGHT. The point is that the light can come from ABOVE, so besides
frontal light or 45 degrees, you can also light from overhead. That gives very beautiful light.

[15:22] LOW ANGLE AND ITS DISTORTION
I am lying on the floor now, working a lower viewpoint — it gives a different perspective.
I now have two models: the other is Nisha, who is assisting Nadia with posing. I have chosen
a white background, with two striplights aimed at the background to make it properly white,
and an octabox lighting the models.
About the perspective: because I am low, the models' LEGS APPEAR LONGER. You may not
always want that, but I want to show you the effect. Sometimes you have shorter models
who then look taller. But be CAREFUL — because the legs lengthen, the upper body becomes
SHORTER. It gets compressed, and that of course causes distortion. So check whether the
model actually likes it. Once in a while it's fine.
[Directs both models — closer together, faces turned, back to back, hand on the chest;
notices it isn't symmetrical and has them swap hands; then lightly hold hands.]
Directing two models is harder than one — it is simply a matter of practice, finding what
works and what doesn't.

[18:33] FOCAL LENGTH TEST: 16mm → 200mm
I have been shooting with my 24-70 the whole time, one of my favourite lenses — but it is
good to shoot with other lenses. I'll show you what different focal lengths do.
Now a wide-angle at 16mm — I'm standing very close. With wide angle you get real
distortion. Now the 24-70 at 24mm. Now 70mm — I take a step back, and another step.
And now we turn it all the way to 200mm and go right to the back. (The tether cable won't
reach, so we unplug it.) From here the models fill the frame completely.
So we have photographed from 16mm all the way to 200mm. You will see what each lens
does to the body — you really get distortion — and you will notice that at 200mm you get
the most beautiful result.
TIP: Where you stand and which lens you use determines your photograph. Search for the
best result TOGETHER WITH your model. You don't have to reinvent the wheel — use photo
books for inspiration and consult with your model.

[20:45] BACKGROUNDS — WHITE, AND METERING IT
Backgrounds can considerably improve your photograph, or even ruin it if the background
doesn't cooperate. In the studio I have black, white, red, yellow — many colours — but I work
mostly with black and white.
To use a white background properly you must light the background well AND your subject.
I light the background with two striplights aimed at it. You don't have to use striplights —
other modifiers work — I use them because I use them for other things anyway.
What matters: there should be about TWO STOPS of difference between your background
and your subject. Let's meter the background with the light meter: about f/11.5. Which
means my subject should sit around f/5.6-6.5. Let's meter — yes, about f/5.6-6.5. So the
subject is correctly exposed and so is the background.
Light also falls on Nisha, and for that I use this OCTABOX — a softbox in an eight-sided
shape, which you can see from its eight corners. The light comes from this side, so Nisha is
lit from this side. But she has another side too, and we light that very slightly with this very
large reflector board: a little light from the octabox and a little from the background
reflects off it and lands on Nisha.

[23:03] ONE BACKGROUND, THREE LOOKS
I've placed Nadia on the beam. Now — shall I show you that I can give the background a
different colour? Nisha, can you help me switch that lamp off?
I have changed NOTHING in the settings. I shoot at exactly the same setting. And now you
see we have a GREY background. So with a white background you can also make a grey one.

[23:52] BLACK BACKGROUND, LOW KEY, PROPS
Now the black background — one of my favourites, because you can make beautiful mood
images with it. I call them low-key shots.
Something else that's nice: this is a PLANT SPRAYER with water in it, and we're going to
spray some droplets on Nisha. What you get is a more DYNAMIC effect — the droplets sit
beautifully on the body and give a kind of 3D effect. I think it's wonderful. You don't have to
always do it, but if you want something extra, use water droplets. (I explain it first rather
than doing it straight away — you shouldn't leave your model standing in the cold.)
[Sprays, shoots.] That gives a more dynamic effect.
[25:09] Nadia now has BOXING GLOVES on. When you are doing a nude photo you don't
necessarily have to stay nude the whole time — you can add PROPS. With the boxing gloves
you have something extra in the photo, but beyond that Nadia perhaps feels slightly less
exposed. And it simply adds something nice.
[25:34] Earlier I showed that a white background can be made grey by switching off the REAR
lamps. Now I have left the rear lamps ON and switched the FRONT lamp OFF. What do you
get? "Nadia, stand as if you have just won the match — you have great power." And what do
you get? A SILHOUETTE. So still with the white background we have created completely
different things.
TIP: If you cannot afford expensive backgrounds, go to the market for some fabric, or try the
hardware store for rolls of wallpaper.

[26:40] THE KIT
I am not in favour of immediately buying and using expensive equipment.
- A BACKGROUND SYSTEM with rolls on pulleys; I have three colours hanging.
- A LAMP: this is a studio strobe that also has continuous light — a 1000W continuous lamp
  inside. The brand doesn't really matter as long as it gives you flash or continuous light.
- LIGHT MODIFIERS (light shapers), because a lamp emits light but you must shape it:
  * REFLECTOR — reflects and bundles the light into a beam toward background or model.
  * REFLECTOR WITH HONEYCOMB GRID — lets you aim the light more; think of small tubes
    directing all the rays one way. Handy when you want narrower beams.
  * SNOOT — narrower still, concentrates the light further; also takes a honeycomb filter to
    direct it even more. These all make the light HARD.
  * SOFTBOX — for soft light; especially beautiful for model photography.
  * UMBRELLA — essentially similar to a softbox, also soft light. Comes white-lined or
    silver-lined, and there is the shoot-through type where you photograph through it. The
    key difference: a SOFTBOX DIRECTS the light more, an UMBRELLA SPREADS it more.
  * BEAUTY DISH — much used for beauty portraits but good for all model photography.
    Versus a softbox: round shape rather than rectangular, so you get a CIRCLE in the eyes
    rather than a rectangle — more the association with the sun, which is why many
    photographers use one.
- REFLECTOR BOARD — a 5-in-1: silver side, matte gold side, true gold side (not real gold,
  gold-coloured), white side, and the inner diffusion screen which you can hold in front of
  your lamps to soften the light. With the board you STEER the light: use silver to add light
  on the model; if that is too much, use matte gold — though gold makes it warmer. And if
  you want LESS light — say you're doing low-key — use a BLACK screen to BLOCK light.
- CAMERA + LENSES: an SLR with the 24-70 f/2.8, a lovely range and one of my favourites.
  Also a 70-200 for more reach (harder in a home studio, but fine outdoors or in a larger
  space); a 100mm MACRO, a beautiful portrait lens and ideal when you want to photograph
  skin detail or water droplets very close; an 85mm f/1.2, so fast you can hold only the eyes
  sharp with everything else soft, and lovely for parts of the body with shallow depth; and
  an 11-16mm wide angle if you want to get a bit crazy — you can take in more, but be
  careful, you get distortion.
- TRIGGERS: a transmitter and receiver set (mine is a Cactus set — just a brand) so you can
  fire your lamps WIRELESSLY. You can use a cable, but wireless simply works better.
- LIGHT METER: the difference from the meter in your camera is that this measures INCIDENT
  light, while the camera measures REFLECTED light — which can confuse the camera. With
  flash you can fire and fire until the exposure is right, and read off the aperture you need.
- LENS HOODS: the name says it — often used outdoors against the sun. But do I only use it
  outdoors? No, I always use it in the studio too, because there is light in the studio as well,
  reflecting back off your lamps or the background. It nicely blocks that light and prevents
  internal reflection in your camera and lens.
TIP: Don't invest in the most expensive gear straight away — grow along with your equipment.

[34:34] ABSTRACT WORK — MACRO AND FORM
When photographing people you're quickly inclined to put the whole person in frame —
maybe a half-body, or a portrait. But it is also enjoyable to make more ABSTRACT images.
Setup: a striplight on the left lighting this side; a striplight behind with a grid in front of it to
direct the light more, giving a rim light effect; and a lamp with a reflector on the background
producing a kind of circle back there, which I simply find attractive.
Lens: 100mm macro f/2.8 — fast. The beauty of the macro is that you can shoot very close
and capture real detail in the skin.
Settings: ISO 400, 1/250, f/2.8. Why f/2.8? For a beautiful shallow-depth effect — very little
in the frame is sharp, so you get a lovely falloff. Why 1/250? I'm at 100mm and want to shoot
handheld; I could bring the shutter closer to 1/100, but to stay safe and take no risks I use
1/250 — which is why I set ISO 400 rather than lower. With the current generation of digital
SLRs you needn't worry about ISO 400.
[36:12] Now, CHOCOLATE SAUCE — we pour a little over her body. A small amount; less is
more, keep it subtle. The sauce runs beautifully.
That gives more of Nadia's form, but it's also good to go closer and see whether we can make
something abstract. With the macro you can make thoroughly defamiliarized images where
you genuinely think "what IS that?" — and with f/2.8 very little is sharp, so you get a strange
but very interesting picture. The body has many forms, so it is interesting to go looking for
what is possible.
[37:15] I noticed Nadia has a beautiful piercing, and I've taken a chain from the box to work
with. It already looks interesting, but I'll go in very close with the macro. Good result — yet
I'm missing something. I'll add WATER DROPLETS. [Sprays.] The droplets give that little extra.
Now the photograph has just that bit more, which for me finishes it.
[38:30] I've shot on a dark background and a brown one; now on to white. I want to show that
you shouldn't let yourself be limited — don't be satisfied too quickly when working a black
background; look at what else is possible. Nadia is lying beautifully on the floor and I'll
photograph her only from the waist to the upper thigh. Focus carefully. This again gives a
stranger, more unusual image where you think "what is that?" — and that is precisely what
makes it interesting.
TIP: The line between EROTICISM, AESTHETICS and PORNOGRAPHY falls differently in every
culture. Nude photography is a form of aesthetic suggestion. Through beautiful poses and
surprising crops you as photographer can show how beautiful the forms of a nude body
really are, without it becoming crude.

[39:17] MAKEUP — "SHAPING" IS DODGE AND BURN BEFORE THE SHUTTER
Good makeup matters at a shoot: besides removing blemishes, a good makeup artist can
also SHAPE the body. This is one of my regular artists, Rina.
RINA: I'm removing a small spot on Nadia's body — there was a pimple there, and now there
isn't. I've already done her face and I'm going over the body to see whether there are marks
that can be removed, a small vein, anything else. With nude photography there is much more
involved than just the face. ALWAYS check with the model, or ask whether the model herself
has indicated something she wants removed. I find that very important.
RAOUL: Can you explain and show what shaping is?
RINA: I've already done one of Nadia's breasts; I'll do the other. Looking at them now, what
do you see? — Yes, one appears larger than the other in form. That is shaping: you literally
give FORM to the body. I work with LIGHT and DARK. It is very simple: everything you make
LIGHTER comes FORWARD, and everything you make DARKER falls BACK. I've applied a
contour with a darker eyeshadow — that section falls back. Compared to the shadow, the
skin tone on her chest is lighter, so that comes forward.
RAOUL: This is the dodging and burning we do in Photoshop.
RINA: Exactly. The advantage of the artist having shaped it already is that you don't have to
do it at home. — You can go many directions with the body, perhaps even more than with
the face alone. You can also remove tattoos. Nadia has a tattoo that I have not removed and
won't, but you could remove it if the assignment required it.
RAOUL: And if the photographer cannot arrange a makeup artist?
RINA: A shame, but it happens. Fortunately almost every model knows a little about how to
do her own makeup. What is useful for a photographer to have is a BASIC KIT: mainly a brush
set — don't assume you need every brush, but a foundation brush, a blush brush and a
powder brush, supplemented with a few eyeshadow brushes. What really must not be
missing is the DISPOSABLE SPONGE — working on the BODY with your brushes every time is
not hygienic, so I use a disposable sponge. Also essential: concealers in various tones and
foundations. You don't know whether you'll have a fair model, a tanned model or a very dark
model — so always have DIFFERENT FOUNDATION TONES on hand. Some eyeshadows. And
blushes, in case the model looks a bit pale and you want to bring colour to the face.
TIP: Good makeup matters at a shoot. A made-up woman feels more beautiful and is
therefore more confident on set.

[43:13] POST-PROCESSING
A photo as it rolls out of the camera has average contrast, average sharpness and average
colour — so you should always do something with it. Some people say "photos straight out
of camera are already good, one shot one kill." But if you think about it, photos were always
edited before too: partly in the darkroom, dodging and burning, but also when you took your
film to the print lab — the person behind the machine added a little colour, a little
saturation. So photos were always processed.
[44:56] PHOTO 1 — the ceiling is visible. I remove it with the CLONE TOOL. Cloning says it: you
copy one thing exactly to another point. First make the brush bigger — via the size field, or
with the bracket keys. How the clone tool works: hold ALT and click to choose a SOURCE.
Release alt, then paint over the area with the mouse. Because the mouse keeps moving I
have to take a new SAMPLE now and then — a new source point. [Works it away.] Press F12
to return to how the photo was taken: this is how it was, and this is how it becomes.
[46:38] PHOTO 2 — this one already looks fine, but it would be interesting converted to BLACK
AND WHITE, which gives a different mood and appearance. There are various ways; I won't
say which is better, I'll just show mine. Create a new ADJUSTMENT LAYER — click the
half-moon icon and choose SOLID COLOR. Choose BLACK. Now your photo has disappeared —
nothing is wrong. Change the BLEND MODE from Normal to COLOR (it's right at the bottom).
Now the photo is black and white, and it has that bit extra.
[47:53] PHOTO 3 — the chocolate sauce shot. It looks good, but zoom in and there are
blemishes; here a streak runs across. Maybe Nadia sat on something — it doesn't matter, we
just remove it. I use the SPOT HEALING BRUSH, also resized with the bracket keys. It is an
easier version of the clone tool: put it on the blemish and move over it. You can do the whole
streak at once, but it's good to pause now and then (partly because my computer isn't fast).
The streak disappears. You only have to tap and move over it — Photoshop does the thinking.
Some photographers say you must remove nothing, the person is the person. In principle I
agree that you should not DEFORM people — leave people as they are — but I think TEMPORARY
blemishes should simply be removed. Here is a piercing: that belongs to Nadia, so of course
we leave it. [Removes it as a demonstration.] Funny, isn't it — but we'll put it back, because it
belongs to her.
Zooming out, I think this is her arm. I could retouch it away, but what I can also do is simply
CROP the photo — I think the image becomes a lot more interesting. See? Now you have a
square crop, and I find the image finished. F12 to go back to the opening state: this was it,
and this is what it becomes — the streaks gone, and a new crop. So a photo that already
looked fine gets, with a small investment of time and effort, that bit extra.
TIP: After processing your photo looks good — but that is only the beginning. Make sure your
photos are SEEN in various forms: have your own website, keep a portfolio book, or enter
competitions. From my own experience you can have quite some luck with those.
