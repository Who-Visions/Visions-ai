# Posing Curves — Sue Bryce's Three-Move System

Source: video masterclass transcript, ingested 2026-09-03. **Presenter: Sue Bryce** — the
transcript itself names no one, but the attribution is sound on three independent grounds:
the source was found via a YouTube search for her name; the presenter is a New Zealander who
references shooting two sisters in Auckland; and "chin forward and down" plus the cover-girl
frame are her signature system. Video: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FWvQUhzdtFA>
(`transcripts/FWvQUhzdtFA.json` carries the structured record; this document is the prose
breakdown and the two should stay in sync).

> "We're not trying to get everyone to mold into an ideal. We're just trying to make
> everyone look the best they can be."

---

## The three moves — everything else is variation

Applied to **every** body type, not only curves. The presenter states plainly that she poses
everybody this way.

1. **45 degrees to camera.** Not side-on — 45. Opens the shoulder and pushes it out and back.
2. **Weight on the back foot.** Pushes the near hip away from the lens. Then push the weight
   into the *front* of the hip and it travels back further still.
3. **Chin forward and down.** The signature move. **The curvier the body, the further forward
   and down it has to go.**

Say it out loud to the client: *"Feels weird, looks fantastic."* She uses that line constantly.

---

## Camera position — the thing most people get backwards

**Shoot on her eye line, or a touch above it. Never from up high.**

> "Everybody has this idea that we're photographing up here. This is so 90s. We don't do that
> anymore. It looks wrong, and it's not how you slim somebody down, because all they do is
> look like they're looking up at you."

Slimming comes from **tilting the body**, not from raising the camera. A hair above the eye
line is useful because it engages chin-forward-and-down; anything more is the 90s mistake.

**Lens:** shoot the **35mm**. Slightly wide, so focus holds on the face and the body drops
away — subtle and genuinely slimming. **The 50 reads flatter.**

**The exception that matters:** on a very **lean** body, do NOT pull the hips away from
camera. It makes the head look huge.

---

## Hands and arms — where the bulk is won

**Arms resting against the body read as one mass with it.** Get them off the torso and the
body instantly narrows.

- **Give the hands a job.** Thumbs into the jean pockets does three things at once: relaxes
  the hands, shapes the elbows, and lifts the arms off the ribcage.
- **"Barbie hands" / tabletop hands.** Hands out flat as if resting on a table, both sides —
  then **pull the elbows back behind the body.** Narrows the whole body line and brings the
  chest line forward. This is her cover-girl setup.
- **Failure mode:** shoulders pulled back *too* far. Pull the elbows, not the shoulders.
- **Tucking the front arm.** Have her roll toward you until the front arm drops straight into
  the wall line. Everybody wants good arms — once the arm looks good, everything does.
- **Dominant forearm.** If a forearm leads the frame, drop it, or move inside the forearm line.
- **Hands low, never stiff or splayed** in front.
- **Make triangles.** Space you can see through between arm and body is what reads as slim.
  The more triangles, the more space, the more slimming.

**Chest:** bring the arm line in and it simultaneously slims the arms and cuts the bust line —
*"boobs are squishy and they move really well."* Don't over-project the chest; if it pushes too
far forward, pull back through the core, almost sinking down.

---

## The faux waist — the strongest single trick here

When you ask someone to put their hands on their waist, **they put them on their true waist.
Don't let them.** Place the hands **higher and further in** and invent a waistline.

> "The eye follows this. Instantly the eye picks this up as being her new waistline."

Then **kick the booty back** and push the chin forward. Hands wide leaves distance that makes
the waist read wider than it is; hands in and up define it.

Watch that she isn't **pinching fabric** at the front.

> "No slimming in Photoshop needed, because it's all in camera."

---

## Eliminating the double chin

> "Nobody will buy a portrait with a double chin. You won't. I won't. It is that simple, and
> it is so easy to eliminate."

- Chin **forward and down** — create visible **space between the chin and the chest line**.
- **Push the shoulder down.** The lower that shoulder goes, the longer the neck reads.
- Counterintuitive check: in a relaxed neck position the chin often *photographs* as though
  it's raised even when it doesn't look raised to your eye. Trust the camera, not the room.

**Stopping rapid blinking:** tell her to *"slow down your blinks."* Naming it makes her aware,
and the rapid blinking stops.

---

## Seated

- **Slide the near arm against the thigh and all the way back** — instantly defines the hourglass.
- **Knees together**, then take knees *and feet* away from camera. The further the lower half
  travels, the longer and slimmer she reads, and the more the frame concentrates on the face.
- Keep a **triangle** at the front hand — light through the gap.
- **Push the bum back** on the box. More recline, more shape.
- **Don't let the shot fold up.** Crowding is the enemy; stretch the whole body out.

## The apple box against a wall

Sitting on the **floor** you cannot recline — everything you do shortens the torso. An apple
box lifts her so the body opens.

Sit on one hip, shoulder back to the wall, lean back, knees together, elbow away from the body
and pressed to the wall. Drop the shoulder lower and the chin/shoulder line lengthens.

**Her contrarian call:** shooting slightly down at a properly reclined curvy subject works —
*if* the recline and the chin-down are real. Sitting bolt upright because it feels more
slimming is where everybody goes wrong.

## Lying down

On the tummy, elbows forward, scooch back, feet up over the back of the couch. Right arm
forward, left across. Lift tall, chin around and down. Then: elbow out, **fingertips touching
the back of the hair**, chin around and down.

**Soft couches are hard** — she sinks in and can't hold a long posture. Demand stretch and height.

## Over the shoulder

**With curves there must be a whole-body tip forward.** And the crucial distinction:

> "It's not about tipping her forward to the camera. It's actually about tipping her forward
> this way, so that I can drop that down."

The tip is along her own axis, which opens the neck and shoulders and gives the face angle.
The common error is simply dropping the shoulder instead of the engaged over-shoulder look.

---

## Lighting noted in passing

Cover-girl frames shot **straight into backlight** with a reflector bouncing back to the face.
A black v-flat for the milky-skinned subject in red.

---

## How she directs

> "Your client is waiting for you to give them guidance, because they can't see through the
> camera. You can."

> "We don't just bang out 500 shots and hope that we get one. We sculpt them, move them."

Constant verbal resets, every frame: stand tall through the neck and shoulders, chin forward
and down, weight onto the back foot. Then re-adjust *your* position as well as hers.

Her own default whenever a camera appears: weight on the back foot, waist defined, chin
forward and down.

---

## The ethic, which she treats as the real lesson

She photographed two sisters in Auckland and shot only head-and-shoulders. Afterwards they
asked her why. Three reasons, and the third is the one that matters:

1. She didn't yet know how to photograph bodies well.
2. She had no retouching to fall back on.
3. **She was judging their bodies through her own** — she had gained weight in her late
   twenties and was avoiding cameras herself.

> "Never assume for one minute that they're not proud and happy with who they are and what
> they are, just because I wasn't."

> "I don't care what their skin type is, if they've got scars or birthmarks. Everybody wants
> to look and feel fabulous for them."

**The commercial fact underneath it:** people buy photographs in which *they* look good. The
mother buys the family portrait if the mother looks good — everyone else's frame is secondary.
Making the subject look incredible is not vanity work; it is the business.
